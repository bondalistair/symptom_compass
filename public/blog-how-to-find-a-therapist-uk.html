How to Find a Therapist in the UK: What to Look For and What to Ask

By The Symptom Compass Team

Finding a therapist can feel like a big task when you're already struggling. This guide walks you through it step by step: deciding between NHS and private support, understanding qualifications and registers, what to ask before you start, red flags to look out for, and how to tell whether a therapist is the right fit for you.

Deciding to look for a therapist is an important step. But once you start searching, it's easy to feel overwhelmed by the different titles, approaches, directories and prices.

The good news is that you don't need to get everything right first time. This guide breaks the process into manageable steps, so you can make a choice you feel confident about. It focuses on the UK, where some important rules about therapist titles may surprise you.

Step 1: Think About What You're Looking For

Before you start searching, it can help to think about a few things:

  • What's troubling you most, and what would you like to change?
  • Would you prefer practical tools, a space to talk, or a chance to understand where your difficulties come from?
  • Do you have a preference for online or in-person sessions, or for a therapist of a particular gender, background or language?
  • How much can you afford, and how often could you attend?

If you're not sure which type of therapy might suit you, our guide Types of Therapy Explained explains the main approaches. Symptom Compass can also suggest directions worth exploring based on what you describe.

Step 2: Decide Between NHS and Private Support

  • NHS: therapy is free, but you usually have less choice over the type of therapy and the therapist, and there may be a wait. In England, you can refer yourself to NHS Talking Therapies for common problems like anxiety and depression. Elsewhere in the UK, your GP is usually the place to start.
  • Private: you can choose your therapist and approach, and waits are often shorter, but you pay for each session. Some private health insurance policies cover therapy.
  • Other routes: workplaces, universities and charities sometimes offer free or low-cost counselling.

For a step-by-step guide to the NHS route in England, read How to Self-Refer for NHS Talking Therapies. The rest of this guide is especially useful if you're looking privately.

Step 3: Understand Titles and Qualifications

This is the part that surprises many people. In the UK, the titles "counsellor", "psychotherapist" and "therapist" are not protected by law. That means anyone can use them, whatever their training.

Some titles are protected. The Health and Care Professions Council (HCPC), the statutory regulator, protects nine titles for practitioner psychologists, including clinical psychologist and counselling psychologist. Anyone using one of these titles must be on its register. Arts therapists, such as art psychotherapists, dramatherapists and music therapists, are also regulated by the HCPC. Psychiatrists are medical doctors registered with the General Medical Council (GMC).

For counsellors and psychotherapists, the main safeguard is the Accredited Registers programme run by the Professional Standards Authority (PSA). The PSA is an independent body that checks whether professional registers meet its standards. A therapist on a PSA-accredited register has met that register's training standards, agreed to follow its code of ethics, and can be complained about if something goes wrong.

Look before you book: whoever you choose, check that they're registered with the HCPC or on a PSA-accredited register. You can search all of these in one place on the PSA's "Check your practitioner" page.

Common Registers to Look For

These are some of the PSA-accredited registers you're likely to come across, listed alphabetically. Being on any one of them is a good sign. No single register is "better" than the others, and many therapists belong to more than one.

  • British Association for Counselling and Psychotherapy (BACP)
  • British Psychoanalytic Council (BPC)
  • CBT Register UK (run jointly by the BABCP and AREBT, for CBT practitioners)
  • National Counselling and Psychotherapy Society (NCPS)
  • UK Council for Psychotherapy (UKCP)

For psychologists and arts therapists, check the HCPC register. For psychiatrists, check the GMC register.

Step 4: Where to Look

  • Search the online directories run by the registers themselves. Most let you search by location, approach and online availability.
  • Use the PSA's "Check your practitioner" page to confirm a therapist you've found elsewhere.
  • Ask your GP, or check whether your employer, university or insurer offers a list of therapists.
  • If you use a commercial therapy directory or app, still check the therapist's registration yourself. Being listed on a directory is not the same as being on an accredited register.

Step 5: Questions to Ask Before You Start

Most therapists are happy to have a short call or exchange emails before you book. Some offer a free introductory chat. Questions you might ask include:

  • What are your qualifications, and which register are you on?
  • What type of therapy do you offer, and how might it help with what I'm experiencing?
  • Do you have experience of working with difficulties like mine?
  • What happens in a typical session, and how long will we work together, roughly?
  • How much do you charge, and what's your cancellation policy?
  • Do you offer online, phone or in-person sessions?
  • How do you handle confidentiality, and when might you need to share information?
  • How will we know whether therapy is helping?

A good therapist will answer openly and won't mind you asking. If they can't help with your particular difficulty, they should be honest and may suggest someone who can.

Step 6: Trust Your Sense of Fit

Research consistently suggests that the relationship between you and your therapist, sometimes called the therapeutic alliance, matters whichever type of therapy you have. After your first session or two, ask yourself:

  • Did I feel listened to and respected?
  • Did I feel comfortable enough to be honest, even if it was difficult?
  • Did the therapist explain how they work in a way I understood?
  • Do I have a sense of what we're working towards?

It's normal to feel nervous at first, and therapy can sometimes feel uncomfortable. That doesn't mean it isn't working. But if something consistently doesn't feel right, it's OK to raise it with your therapist, or to look for someone else.

Red Flags to Look Out For

Most therapists are ethical and professional. But be cautious if a therapist:

  • can't or won't tell you which register they're on, or which qualifications they hold
  • promises a cure or guaranteed results
  • pressures you to book or pay for lots of sessions upfront
  • dismisses your concerns, or makes you feel judged or blamed
  • talks a lot about themselves or their own problems
  • doesn't explain confidentiality, or shares information about other clients
  • suggests a friendship, business relationship or any kind of romantic or physical relationship
  • discourages you from seeking other medical help when you need it

If you're worried about a therapist's behaviour, you can stop seeing them at any time. You can also complain to the register they belong to, or to the HCPC or GMC if they're regulated.

If you need help right now: if you feel unable to keep yourself safe, call 999 or go to A&E. For urgent help that isn't an emergency, call NHS 111 (in England, choose the mental health option). You can call Samaritans free on 116 123, any time of day or night.

What Are Your Options?

Different practitioners have different training and are checked in different ways. This table gives a quick overview:

PractitionerWhat they usually doHow to check them
CounsellorTalking therapy, often focused on current difficulties; may use one or several approachesTitle not protected; check for a PSA-accredited register, such as BACP or NCPS
PsychotherapistTalking therapy, sometimes longer-term or exploring deeper patterns; trained in one or more approachesTitle not protected; check for a PSA-accredited register, such as UKCP, BPC or BACP
CBT therapistStructured cognitive behavioural therapyTitle not protected; check for the CBT Register UK (BABCP/AREBT) or another accredited register
Clinical or counselling psychologistAssessment and psychological therapy, often for more complex difficultiesProtected titles; check the HCPC register
Arts therapistTherapy using art, drama or musicProtected titles; check the HCPC register
PsychiatristMedical doctor who can diagnose and prescribe medication, usually through the NHS or private clinicsCheck the GMC register

Many therapists are trained in more than one approach. The title matters less than whether the person is properly trained, registered and a good fit for you.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if a therapist is qualified?

Ask which register they belong to, then check it yourself. Psychologists and arts therapists should be on the HCPC register. Counsellors and psychotherapists should ideally be on a PSA-accredited register, which you can search on the PSA's "Check your practitioner" page.

Is a counsellor the same as a psychotherapist?

The roles overlap a lot. Counselling is often shorter and focused on a current difficulty, while psychotherapy may be longer or explore deeper patterns. In the UK, neither title is protected by law, so checking registration matters more than the title.

Does the type of therapy matter, or the therapist?

Both can matter. For some conditions, NICE (the independent body that produces treatment guidelines for the NHS) recommends particular approaches. But research also suggests the relationship with your therapist plays an important part, whatever the approach.

Can I change therapist if it isn't working?

Yes. It's common and completely acceptable. You might first talk to your therapist about what isn't working, as that conversation can sometimes help. But you're always free to look for someone else.

Is online therapy as good as face-to-face?

Many people find online therapy works well, and it can be more convenient. Some prefer being in the same room. It's mostly a matter of what feels right for you, and of your circumstances.

Is Symptom Compass a diagnosis?

No. Symptom Compass suggests therapy directions to explore based on what you describe. It isn't a diagnosis, and it doesn't replace an assessment by a qualified professional.
