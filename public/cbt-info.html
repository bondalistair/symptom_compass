What is CBT?

Cognitive Behavioral Therapy — one of the most researched and widely used talking therapies.

The basic idea

CBT is built on a simple observation: the way we think about a situation shapes how we feel about it and how we act — and that chain can work in the other direction too. Changing an unhelpful thought pattern or behavior can, over time, change how you feel.

Rather than spending a lot of time on the distant past, CBT tends to focus on what's happening right now — the specific thoughts, feelings, and habits that are keeping a problem going — and works on shifting those directly.

What a course usually looks like

CBT is typically structured and time-limited — often somewhere between 6 and 20 sessions, though this varies by person and provider. Sessions are usually collaborative: you and the therapist set an agenda together, review how the previous week went, and work on specific techniques.

  • Identifying unhelpful thinking patterns (like catastrophizing or all-or-nothing thinking)
  • Testing those thoughts against real evidence
  • Gradually facing avoided situations rather than avoiding them
  • Building small, practical habits between sessions ("homework")

What it's commonly used for

CBT has one of the largest evidence bases of any talking therapy. It's most established for anxiety disorders, depression, panic disorder, OCD, phobias, and insomnia, and is also used alongside other treatment for a range of other conditions.

What to expect from an initial assessment

A first appointment with a CBT therapist is usually a conversation, not a test. They'll ask about what's been going on, how long it's been happening, and what you'd like to be different — and talk through whether CBT looks like a good fit before any structured work begins.
