Why We Delay Mental Health Treatment: The Hidden Cost of a Confusing System

By Alistair Bond, Senior CBT Therapist

Most people don't put off therapy because they don't want help. They put it off because they don't know where to start. Faced with a maze of therapy types, practitioner titles, waiting lists, and unclear costs, many people simply delay, sometimes for years, until symptoms become harder to manage. Understanding why this happens, and what can be done about it, is an important step toward closing the gap between needing support and actually getting it.

The Scale of the Problem

Delayed treatment is far more common than many people realise. Roughly one in five people experience a mental health condition in a given year, yet less than half who need care actually receive it (1). For some conditions, the average delay between first symptoms and starting treatment is measured in years rather than months: one study of adults with obsessive-compulsive disorder found an average delay of over five years before starting psychotherapy, with a lack of knowledge about treatment options cited as the most common barrier (2). Similar patterns appear across depression and anxiety, where people frequently describe a long gap between recognising something is wrong and actually reaching a therapist's office (3).

Why "Not Knowing Where to Start" Is a Bigger Problem Than It Sounds

It's tempting to assume the biggest barrier to therapy is stigma or cost, and both matter enormously. But confusion about the process itself is a distinct and often underestimated obstacle. Interviews with people living with depression describe a frustrating trial-and-error approach to finding a suitable provider, often having to explain their history repeatedly to different practitioners before finding the right fit, which can be discouraging enough to make people give up altogether (3). Uncertainty about the process, not knowing what type of therapy suits a problem, what qualifications matter, or even how to book a first appointment, is consistently identified as a factor that delays care (4).

The Practical Barriers That Compound the Confusion

Even once someone decides to seek help, several practical hurdles tend to stack on top of the initial confusion:

  • Waiting lists. Every day of waiting increases the chance someone abandons care altogether. Research suggests that for every additional day of wait time, roughly one percent of patients give up seeking help entirely (5).
  • Cost. Financial strain is one of the most cited reasons for delay. In one U.S. study, nearly a third of adults with depression who also carried medical debt said cost caused them to postpone mental health care (6).
  • Time. Busy schedules are a genuine obstacle, not just an excuse. One review found that around 41% of college students cited a simple lack of time as a reason for not seeking help (6).
  • Provider shortages. Millions of people live in areas with too few therapists, which can mean waiting months for an appointment or travelling long distances for care (6).

The Cost of Waiting

Delay is rarely neutral. One survey found that 80% of people experienced a deterioration in their mental health while waiting for support to begin (7). The consequences extend beyond wellbeing too: research has linked even a one-month delay in treatment to a measurable increase in job loss among affected patients (7). The longer the gap between recognising a problem and receiving appropriate care, the more entrenched symptoms can become, and the harder they can be to treat.

Making the First Step Easier

A few things consistently help people move from "I should probably get help" to actually booking an appointment:

  • Start with the problem, not the label. Describing symptoms in plain language, rather than trying to self-diagnose the right modality, makes it easier to be matched with an appropriate approach.
  • Improve mental health literacy early. Better public understanding of symptoms and treatment options has been shown to shorten the delay to recognition and treatment, particularly among younger people (2).
  • Use tools that simplify the first decision. Structured tools that translate symptoms into likely therapy directions can reduce the guesswork of the first search, making that first step feel far more manageable.

The Bottom Line

Delaying mental health treatment isn't usually a sign of not caring about your wellbeing, it's often a rational response to a genuinely confusing system. Reducing that confusion, through clearer information, simpler first steps, and tools that help people understand their own symptoms, is one of the most effective ways to close the gap between needing support and getting it.
