Panic Attacks: Symptoms, Causes and What Helps

By The Symptom Compass Team

A panic attack can be one of the most frightening experiences there is, especially the first time. This guide explains what panic attacks feel like, why they happen, what you can do in the moment, and how the main treatment options compare, so you can work out what might help you.

If you've ever had a sudden rush of intense fear, with your heart pounding and your breath catching, you may have had a panic attack. Many people say it felt as if they were having a heart attack, losing control, or even dying.

Panic attacks are very frightening, but they aren't dangerous in themselves, and they do pass. They're also very common, and there's a lot that can help.

This article isn't a diagnosis. Only a qualified professional can assess what's going on for you. But it can help you understand what you're experiencing and see the different directions worth exploring.

What Is a Panic Attack?

A panic attack is a sudden surge of intense fear or discomfort, along with strong physical sensations. It's your body's "fight or flight" alarm system going off, even though there's no real danger.

According to the NHS, most panic attacks last between 5 and 20 minutes, although some can last up to an hour. They can happen in response to a stressful situation, or seem to come out of nowhere, even during sleep.

Common Panic Attack Symptoms

Panic attacks affect the body and mind. You might notice:

In your body

  • A racing or pounding heartbeat
  • Chest pain or tightness
  • Shortness of breath, or feeling as if you're choking
  • Dizziness or feeling faint
  • Sweating, hot flushes or chills
  • Trembling or shaky limbs
  • Numbness or pins and needles
  • Feeling sick, a churning stomach or a dry mouth

In your mind

  • A sudden sense of dread or terror
  • Fear of losing control, "going mad" or dying
  • Feeling detached from your body or surroundings
  • An urge to escape

Panic Attack or Something Else?

Because panic attacks cause physical symptoms like chest pain and breathlessness, they can feel like a heart problem. Many of these symptoms can also be caused by other medical conditions.

If you're not sure: if you have chest pain that's severe, crushing or spreading to your arm, jaw, neck or back, if it's the first time you've had symptoms like these, or if you're unsure what's happening, call 999. It's always better to be checked.

You may also hear the term "anxiety attack". It isn't a medical term, and people use it in different ways. Some mean a panic attack. Others mean a slower build-up of intense worry and tension. Panic attacks usually come on suddenly and peak within minutes.

Why Do Panic Attacks Happen?

The exact cause isn't fully understood, and it's often a mix of things:

  • Stress and life events, such as bereavement, work pressure, relationship problems or a traumatic experience.
  • Other anxiety problems, such as generalised anxiety, health anxiety, social anxiety or PTSD.
  • Family history. Panic disorder can run in families.
  • Physical factors, such as caffeine, alcohol, some medicines, recreational drugs and poor sleep, which can trigger or worsen symptoms.
  • The fear of fear. After one panic attack, it's common to start worrying about having another, and to watch your body closely for signs. This worry can itself trigger more attacks.

If you feel anxious much of the time, as well as having panic attacks, you might also find our guide Why Do I Feel Anxious All the Time? helpful.

What Is Panic Disorder?

Having a single panic attack, or a few, doesn't mean you have panic disorder. Panic disorder is when you have regular, unexpected panic attacks, followed by ongoing worry about having more, or changes in how you live to avoid them.

Some people start avoiding places where they've had attacks, or where it might be hard to get away or get help, such as crowded shops, public transport or being far from home. This is sometimes called agoraphobia. A GP can help you work out what's going on.

What to Do During a Panic Attack

Based on NHS advice, these steps can help you ride out a panic attack:

  • Stay where you are, if it's safe to do so.
  • Breathe slowly and deeply. Try breathing in gently through your nose and out slowly through your mouth.
  • Remind yourself that the feelings will pass, and that a panic attack isn't dangerous.
  • Focus on something calming, or on what's around you, such as things you can see, hear and touch.
  • Try not to fight the feelings. Letting them rise and fall can help them pass more quickly.

If someone you're with is having a panic attack, stay calm, speak gently, encourage slow breathing and reassure them that it will pass. Unless there's a medical emergency, avoid crowding them.

When Should I Get Help?

It's a good idea to see a GP or seek support if:

  • you've had a panic attack and aren't sure what caused it, especially if you had chest pain or other physical symptoms
  • panic attacks keep happening
  • you're worrying a lot about having another attack
  • you've started avoiding places or activities because of panic
  • panic is affecting your work, relationships or daily life

In England, you can also refer yourself to NHS Talking Therapies without seeing a GP first.

If you need help right now: if you feel unable to keep yourself safe, call 999 or go to A&E. For urgent help that isn't an emergency, call NHS 111 (in England, choose the mental health option). You can call Samaritans free on 116 123, any time of day or night.

What Are Your Options?

Different treatments suit different people. NICE (the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence, an independent organisation that produces guidelines on which treatments the NHS should offer) says that psychological treatment, medication and self-help have all been shown to help panic disorder, and that the benefits of psychological treatment lasted the longest. Your options should be discussed with you.

Here's an honest look at the main options:

Self-Help and Guided Self-Help

Books, online programmes or courses based on CBT, either on your own or with some support from a practitioner. In England, these are offered through NHS Talking Therapies.

May suit you if: your panic attacks are mild to moderate, you'd like to start quickly, or you prefer working at your own pace. NICE includes self-help among its options for panic disorder.

Things to bear in mind: it needs regular practice and motivation, and may not be enough if panic is severe or has led to a lot of avoidance.

CBT (Cognitive Behavioural Therapy)

Helps you understand the cycle of panic, including how frightening thoughts about physical sensations can fuel an attack. It also helps you gradually face the sensations and situations you've been avoiding, so they lose their power.

May suit you if: you'd like practical tools and a structured approach. NICE recommends CBT as the psychological treatment for panic disorder.

Things to bear in mind: it involves practice between sessions and, gradually, facing feelings or situations you find frightening, which can feel challenging at first.

Medication

Your GP can talk through medication. If medication is chosen for panic disorder, NICE recommends a type of antidepressant called an SSRI. Antidepressants can take several weeks to work fully.

May suit you if: you'd prefer medication, or would like to combine it with therapy.

Things to bear in mind: there can be side effects, so you'll need regular reviews with your GP, and you shouldn't stop suddenly without advice. NICE advises against benzodiazepines (a type of sedative) for treating panic disorder.

Psychodynamic Therapy

Explores how past experiences, relationships and feelings you may not be fully aware of might be linked to your panic.

May suit you if: you want to understand the deeper roots of your anxiety, or your panic seems tied to particular relationships or life events.

Things to bear in mind: the evidence for panic disorder is more limited than for CBT, and it isn't among NICE's recommended treatments for panic disorder.

ACT and Mindfulness-Based Approaches

Help you relate differently to frightening sensations and thoughts, so you're less caught up in fighting them.

May suit you if: you've found that trying to control or avoid panic hasn't worked, or you're drawn to mindfulness.

Things to bear in mind: the evidence for panic disorder is still developing, and these approaches aren't among NICE's recommended treatments for it.

Counselling

Offers a supportive space to talk through what's happening and what might be behind it.

May suit you if: your panic is linked to stress, loss or a difficult life situation, and you want space to talk.

Things to bear in mind: it offers fewer specific techniques for tackling panic, and the evidence for panic disorder is more limited.

How to Work Out What Might Suit You

  • Would I like to start with self-help, or speak to someone straight away?
  • Do I want practical techniques to tackle panic directly, or to understand what's behind it?
  • Would I consider medication, on its own or alongside therapy?
  • Has panic led me to avoid places or activities, and would I like help to face them?
  • Do I need free NHS support, or can I look at private options?

Things That May Help in the Meantime

  • Practising slow breathing when you're calm, so it's easier to use during an attack
  • Regular physical activity
  • Cutting down on caffeine and alcohol, and stopping smoking
  • Keeping to a regular sleep routine
  • Talking to someone you trust about what's been happening
  • Looking at support from charities such as Anxiety UK, No Panic and Mind

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a panic attack hurt me?

Panic attacks are very frightening, but they aren't dangerous in themselves. However, because the symptoms can be similar to other conditions, it's important to get checked by a doctor, especially if it's the first time or you have chest pain.

How long does a panic attack last?

According to the NHS, most panic attacks last between 5 and 20 minutes, although some can last up to an hour.

What's the difference between a panic attack and an anxiety attack?

"Anxiety attack" isn't a medical term. Some people use it to mean a panic attack, and others mean a gradual build-up of worry. Panic attacks usually come on suddenly and peak within minutes.

Can panic attacks happen for no reason?

Yes. Panic attacks can come out of the blue, even when you feel relaxed or are asleep. That doesn't mean something is seriously wrong, but it's worth talking to a GP if it keeps happening.

Will I always have panic attacks?

Not necessarily. Many people find that panic attacks become much less frequent, or stop, with the right support. Everyone is different, so it's best to talk to a professional about what to expect.
