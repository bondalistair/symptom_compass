What Is Psychodynamic Therapy? How It Works and Who It Might Suit

By The Symptom Compass Team

Psychodynamic therapy helps you understand how past experiences and patterns you may not be fully aware of shape how you feel and relate to others now. This guide explains how it works, what sessions are like and what it can help with. It also covers its honest limits and the alternatives, so you can decide whether it might suit you.

Psychodynamic therapy is one of the longest-established forms of talking therapy. It's offered privately, in some NHS services and by many charities. If you're curious about why you feel or react the way you do, or you notice the same patterns coming up again and again, it's an approach you may come across.

This guide gives you a balanced picture: what psychodynamic therapy offers, where it has limits, and what else you might consider.

What Is Psychodynamic Therapy?

Psychodynamic therapy is a talking therapy based on the idea that our early experiences and relationships shape the way we think, feel and relate to others, often in ways we're not fully aware of.

For example, someone who often feels let down by friends or partners might notice, through therapy, a pattern of expecting rejection that began in earlier relationships. Seeing that pattern more clearly can make it easier to respond differently.

The aim is to increase your understanding of yourself, so that difficult feelings and repeating patterns make more sense and have less of a hold on you.

How Psychodynamic Therapy Works

A few features make psychodynamic therapy distinctive:

  • It explores the past as well as the present. It looks at how earlier experiences may be linked to what's happening for you now.
  • It pays attention to feelings you may not be fully aware of. This includes mixed feelings, or ones that are hard to put into words.
  • It notices patterns, including how you relate to others and, sometimes, to your therapist, as this can shed light on relationships elsewhere in your life.
  • It's led by what you bring. Sessions usually follow what's on your mind rather than a fixed agenda.
  • It values the relationship. A trusting relationship with your therapist is seen as central to change.

What Happens in Psychodynamic Therapy Sessions?

Your first session or two usually involve an assessment: talking about what's brought you to therapy, your background and relationships, and what you'd like to get from it. You and your therapist will also agree how you'll work together.

Later sessions are usually more open and conversational than in structured therapies. You might:

  • talk about whatever feels most important that week
  • explore feelings, memories, dreams or recurring thoughts
  • notice links between past and present experiences
  • look at patterns in your relationships, including how you feel in therapy itself
  • reflect on what you notice, rather than practising set techniques

There's usually no homework, although you may find yourself reflecting between sessions.

Different Forms of Psychodynamic Therapy

  • Short-term psychodynamic psychotherapy, which focuses on a particular difficulty over a set period.
  • Dynamic interpersonal therapy (DIT), a short-term, structured psychodynamic approach offered in some NHS Talking Therapies services, mainly for depression.
  • Longer-term psychodynamic psychotherapy, which is more open-ended and explores deeper, long-standing patterns.
  • Psychoanalysis, a more intensive form, often with several sessions a week over a long period.
  • Related approaches, such as mentalisation-based treatment and some forms of group, couples and family therapy, which draw on psychodynamic ideas.

What Can Psychodynamic Therapy Help With?

Psychodynamic therapy is used for a wide range of difficulties, including low mood, anxiety, relationship problems, low self-esteem and a sense of being stuck in repeating patterns.

NICE (the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence, an independent organisation that produces guidelines on which treatments the NHS should offer) includes psychodynamic approaches among its options for some conditions:

  • depression, where short-term psychodynamic psychotherapy is one of several options, alongside CBT, behavioural activation, interpersonal therapy, counselling and others
  • social anxiety disorder, where short-term psychodynamic psychotherapy developed for social anxiety is an option for people who decide not to have CBT
  • anorexia in adults, where focal psychodynamic therapy may be considered if other recommended therapies aren't suitable

The research base for psychodynamic therapy is growing, but for many conditions it's smaller than the research base for CBT. That doesn't mean it's less helpful for everyone. It means it has been studied less, particularly in its longer-term forms.

Strengths of Psychodynamic Therapy

  • Helps you understand why you feel and react the way you do, not just what to do about it
  • Can address long-standing patterns in relationships and self-esteem
  • Flexible, following what matters most to you
  • Gives space to explore complex or mixed feelings
  • Some people find its benefits continue after therapy ends

Limits of Psychodynamic Therapy

No therapy suits everyone, and it's worth knowing the limits too:

  • It's less structured, which can feel unsettling if you want clear goals and techniques.
  • It offers fewer practical tools for managing symptoms day to day.
  • Longer-term forms can be a big commitment of time and money, and are mostly available privately.
  • Exploring painful past experiences can feel difficult at times.
  • For some conditions, such as OCD, PTSD or panic disorder, NICE recommends other approaches first.
  • Like any therapy, it doesn't work for everyone, and the skill of the therapist and the relationship with them matter.

Is Psychodynamic Therapy Right for Me?

Psychodynamic therapy may suit you if:

  • you want to understand the roots of your difficulties
  • you notice the same patterns coming up in your relationships or life
  • you'd prefer open conversation to set exercises or homework
  • you're curious about yourself and willing to reflect on difficult feelings

You might prefer a different approach if you'd like practical techniques to tackle a specific problem quickly, or if you have a condition where NICE recommends another therapy first. You can always ask a therapist how they'd work with you.

How to Find Psychodynamic Therapy

  • NHS: some NHS Talking Therapies services in England offer dynamic interpersonal therapy (DIT), and some specialist NHS services offer psychodynamic therapy. Your GP can advise on what's available locally.
  • Private and charity services: check that a therapist is on a PSA-accredited register. Psychodynamic therapists are often registered with the British Psychoanalytic Council (BPC), the UK Council for Psychotherapy (UKCP) or the BACP. Some charities and training organisations offer low-cost psychodynamic therapy.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is psychodynamic therapy the same as psychoanalysis?

Not quite. Both are based on similar ideas, but psychoanalysis is usually more intensive, with several sessions a week over a long period. Psychodynamic therapy is usually weekly and can be short-term or longer-term.

Do I have to talk about my childhood?

Psychodynamic therapy often explores early experiences, but you go at your own pace, and you won't be made to talk about anything you're not ready for. Many sessions focus on what's happening now and how it connects to the past.

Is psychodynamic therapy better or worse than CBT?

Neither is best for everyone. CBT has a larger research base for many conditions and is more structured. Psychodynamic therapy focuses more on understanding deeper patterns. NICE lists both among the options for depression. The right choice depends on your difficulty, your preferences and the therapist.

Will my therapist just listen without saying anything?

No. Psychodynamic therapists usually listen carefully and may say less than in some other therapies, but they'll reflect on what you say, offer thoughts and help you make connections. If you'd like more input, you can say so.

Is psychodynamic therapy available on the NHS?

Sometimes. Some NHS Talking Therapies services in England offer dynamic interpersonal therapy (DIT), and some specialist services offer psychodynamic therapy. Availability varies by area, so ask your GP or local service.
