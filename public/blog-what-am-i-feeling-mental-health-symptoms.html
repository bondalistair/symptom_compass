What Am I Feeling? Common Mental Health Symptoms and Where to Start

By The Symptom Compass Team

Sometimes you just know something isn't right, but you can't put a name to it. This guide walks through common mental health symptoms, including what they can feel like, what they might be linked to, and where you could start, so you can make sense of what's happening and find support that fits.

Many people come to mental health support without a clear label for what they're going through. You might feel flat, wound up, exhausted or overwhelmed, or simply "not yourself". That's completely normal. You don't need to know what's wrong before you're allowed to look for help.

Symptoms rarely come one at a time. Low mood and anxiety often overlap, poor sleep can make everything feel harder, and stress can show up in your body as much as your mind. So rather than trying to fit yourself into a box, it can help to notice what you're experiencing and how much it's affecting your life.

This guide isn't a diagnosis, and it can't tell you exactly what's going on. Only a qualified professional can assess that. What it can do is help you put words to how you feel and see which directions might be worth exploring.

Before You Start: A Few Things Worth Knowing

  • Everyone has difficult days. Feeling sad, anxious or stressed sometimes is part of being human. It becomes more worth paying attention to when it lasts, keeps coming back, or gets in the way of your daily life.

  • Physical health matters too. Some physical conditions, medicines, alcohol and drugs can cause symptoms that feel emotional, such as tiredness, a racing heart or low mood. That's one reason it's worth talking to your GP, especially if symptoms are new or unexplained.

  • You can have more than one thing going on. Many people notice a mix of the symptoms below. That's common, and support can address several at once.

  • What is NICE? You'll see NICE mentioned in this guide. The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence is an independent organisation that reviews the research evidence and produces guidelines on which treatments the NHS should offer for particular conditions. A NICE recommendation applies to a specific condition, not to every problem, and it doesn't mean other approaches can't help.

Common Symptoms and What They Might Mean

Feeling Anxious, Worried or On Edge

What it can feel like: Constant worrying, feeling tense or restless, a sense that something bad will happen, trouble relaxing, and physical signs like a racing heart or tight muscles.

What it might be linked to: ongoing stress, generalised anxiety, health worries, social anxiety, or the after-effects of difficult experiences.

Where you could start: talk to your GP or, in England, refer yourself to NHS Talking Therapies. For generalised anxiety, NICE recommends starting with guided self-help, then CBT, applied relaxation or medication if needed.

Read more: Why Do I Feel Anxious All the Time?

Sudden Waves of Panic

What it can feel like: Sudden, intense fear with a pounding heart, breathlessness, dizziness, shaking or chest tightness, sometimes feeling like you're losing control or something terrible is happening.

What it might be linked to: panic attacks or panic disorder, high stress, or other anxiety problems. Because the physical symptoms can be frightening, it's sensible to have them checked by a GP.

Where you could start: your GP or NHS Talking Therapies. For panic disorder, NICE recommends CBT, with medication as an alternative.

Low Mood or Losing Interest

What it can feel like: Feeling sad, empty or hopeless most of the time, losing interest in things you used to enjoy, low energy, changes in sleep or appetite, and finding everyday tasks harder.

What it might be linked to: depression, grief, stress, burnout, loneliness or physical health problems.

Where you could start: your GP or NHS Talking Therapies. For depression, NICE lists a range of options, including guided self-help, CBT, behavioural activation, interpersonal therapy, counselling, short-term psychodynamic psychotherapy, group exercise and mindfulness, as well as medication.

Read more: Am I Depressed or Just Low?

Feeling Numb, Flat or Disconnected

What it can feel like: Feeling emotionally empty, cut off from yourself or others, as though you're going through the motions, or that things around you don't feel quite real.

What it might be linked to: low mood or depression, high stress, exhaustion, or the effects of trauma. It can also be a way the mind protects itself when things feel too much.

Where you could start: your GP, especially if it's persistent or came on after a difficult experience. Talking therapy can help you understand what's behind it.

Overthinking and Racing Thoughts

What it can feel like: Going over the same thoughts again and again, replaying conversations, struggling to switch off, or finding it hard to make decisions.

What it might be linked to: anxiety, stress, low mood, perfectionism or difficult life situations.

Where you could start: self-help resources can be a good first step. If it's affecting your sleep or daily life, talking therapies such as CBT, ACT or mindfulness-based approaches may be worth exploring.

Unwanted, Intrusive Thoughts

What it can feel like: Upsetting thoughts, images or urges that pop into your mind, feel out of character, and are hard to shake. Some people feel driven to check, clean, seek reassurance or repeat actions to ease the anxiety.

What it might be linked to: intrusive thoughts are very common and don't mean you want to act on them. When they're frequent and come with compulsions, they can be part of OCD. They can also be linked to anxiety, perinatal mental health or trauma.

Where you could start: your GP or NHS Talking Therapies. For OCD, NICE recommends CBT that includes exposure and response prevention (ERP), and medication may also be offered.

Struggling After a Traumatic Experience

What it can feel like: Flashbacks, nightmares, feeling constantly on guard, avoiding reminders, feeling numb, or being easily startled after something frightening or distressing.

What it might be linked to: a normal reaction in the first few weeks after trauma. If symptoms last longer or are severe, they may be signs of PTSD.

Where you could start: your GP, or NHS Talking Therapies if you're in England. For PTSD in adults, NICE recommends trauma-focused CBT or EMDR.

Stress, Burnout and Feeling Overwhelmed

What it can feel like: Feeling unable to cope, constantly tired, irritable, unable to switch off from work or responsibilities, and less effective at things you usually manage well.

What it might be linked to: long-term pressure at work, caring or home, often without enough rest or support. Burnout can overlap with anxiety and depression.

Where you could start: look at what's in your control, such as rest, boundaries and support at work. If it's affecting your mood, sleep or health, speak to your GP. Counselling, CBT or ACT may help.

Problems Sleeping

What it can feel like: Difficulty getting to sleep or staying asleep, waking very early, or still feeling tired after rest. Your mind may race as soon as your head hits the pillow.

What it might be linked to: anxiety, low mood, stress, physical health problems, caffeine, alcohol or irregular routines.

Where you could start: improving sleep habits is a good first step. If problems carry on, your GP can check for other causes and may suggest a form of CBT designed for insomnia (CBT-I).

Irritability and Anger

What it can feel like: Snapping at people, feeling on a short fuse, getting frustrated easily, or anger that feels out of proportion and hard to control.

What it might be linked to: stress, anxiety, low mood, exhaustion, pain, or difficult experiences that haven't been processed.

Where you could start: notice what tends to trigger it. Talking therapies such as CBT, counselling or psychodynamic therapy can help you understand and manage anger.

Grief That Doesn't Ease

What it can feel like: Intense longing, sadness, guilt or anger after a loss, which isn't easing over time and is making daily life very hard.

What it might be linked to: grief is a natural response to loss and there's no right way to grieve. For some people, it stays very intense for a long time.

Where you could start: bereavement services, charities and counselling can offer support. Your GP can help if grief is affecting your mood, sleep or health.

Drinking or Other Habits Feeling Harder to Control

What it can feel like: Using alcohol, drugs, gambling, food or other habits to cope with feelings, finding it hard to cut down, or worrying about how much you're doing it.

What it might be linked to: stress, anxiety, low mood, trauma or loneliness. Habits that start as a way of coping can become problems in their own right.

Where you could start: your GP or a local drug and alcohol service, many of which accept self-referrals. If you drink heavily every day, don't stop suddenly without medical advice, because it can be dangerous.

Relationship Patterns That Keep Repeating

What it can feel like: Ending up in similar difficult relationships, struggling to trust people, fearing rejection, or repeatedly feeling let down or misunderstood.

What it might be linked to: earlier experiences, long-standing beliefs about yourself and others, or attachment patterns.

Where you could start: approaches that explore patterns over time, such as psychodynamic therapy, schema therapy, interpersonal therapy or couples therapy, may be worth exploring.

If you need help right now: if you're having thoughts of ending your life or harming yourself, or feel unable to keep yourself safe, call 999 or go to A&E. For urgent help that isn't an emergency, call NHS 111 (in England, choose the mental health option). You can call Samaritans free on 116 123, any time of day or night.

When Should I Get Help?

It's worth reaching out if your symptoms:

  • have lasted a few weeks or more, or keep coming back

  • are affecting your work, studies, relationships or day-to-day life

  • are causing you a lot of distress

  • are leading you to avoid people, places or situations

  • are something you're managing with alcohol, drugs or other habits that worry you

You don't need to wait until things feel unbearable, and you don't need to be sure what's wrong before you ask for help.

What Are Your Options?

There's no single right path for everyone. Here's an overview of the main routes and approaches, and how they tend to fit different kinds of symptoms.

Where to Go First

  • Your GP can check for physical causes, talk through your options, refer you on and discuss medication if relevant. This is a good starting point if you're unsure, have physical symptoms, or your symptoms are severe.

  • NHS Talking Therapies (in England) lets you refer yourself for free help with common problems like anxiety and depression. Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have their own services, usually reached through your GP.

  • Private therapy offers more choice of approach and therapist. Check that a therapist is registered with a recognised professional body, such as the BACP, UKCP, BABCP or HCPC.

  • Charities, workplaces and universities often offer free or low-cost support, including counselling, helplines and peer support.

  • Self-help such as NHS Every Mind Matters, can be a useful first step for milder symptoms or while you wait.

How to Work Out What Might Suit You

  • What's affecting me most right now, and what would I most like to change?

  • Would I prefer practical tools, a space to talk, or to understand where this comes from?

  • Do I want to start with self-help, or would I rather speak to someone straight away?

  • Would I consider medication, on its own or alongside therapy?

  • Do I need free NHS support, or can I look at private options?

If you're still unsure, that's fine. A GP or therapist can help you think it through, and it's normal to try more than one approach before finding what fits.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if what I'm feeling is a mental health problem?

There's no simple test. It's worth seeking support if symptoms last for a few weeks or more, keep coming back, cause you a lot of distress, or get in the way of daily life. A GP or qualified professional can help you work out what's going on.

Can mental health symptoms be physical?

Yes. Anxiety, stress and low mood often show up in the body, for example as tiredness, headaches, stomach problems, a racing heart or muscle tension. Because physical health problems can cause similar symptoms, it's worth getting new or unexplained symptoms checked by a GP.

What if I have lots of different symptoms?

That's very common. Many people experience a mix, such as anxiety and low mood together, or stress that affects sleep. A professional can help you understand how they fit together, and treatment can often address several at once.

Do I need a diagnosis to get therapy?

Not necessarily. In England, you can refer yourself to NHS Talking Therapies, and private therapists and counsellors don't usually require a diagnosis. A diagnosis can sometimes help guide treatment, but you can seek support without one.

What if I don't know how to describe what I'm feeling?

That's completely normal. You can start with everyday words, like "I feel flat", "I can't switch off" or "I'm not myself". Professionals are used to helping people find the words.

Is Symptom Compass a diagnosis?

No. Symptom Compass suggests therapy directions to explore based on what you describe. It isn't a diagnosis, and it doesn't replace an assessment by a qualified professional.
