How to Self-Refer for NHS Talking Therapies: A Step-by-Step Guide

By The Symptom Compass Team

If you live in England, you can refer yourself for free NHS talking therapy for problems like anxiety and depression, without seeing a GP first. This guide explains who NHS Talking Therapies is for, how to self-refer step by step, what happens next, and what other options are available, wherever you are in the UK.

Many people don't realise they can refer themselves for NHS therapy. If you've been struggling with your mood, worry or anxiety, and the idea of booking a GP appointment feels like a hurdle, self-referral can be a simpler way to get started.

This guide walks you through the process. It's based on NHS guidance, but local services work slightly differently, so check your local service's website for the details that apply to you.

What Is NHS Talking Therapies?

NHS Talking Therapies, for anxiety and depression, is a free NHS service in England offering psychological therapies for common mental health problems. It used to be called IAPT (Improving Access to Psychological Therapies), and you may still see that name in some places.

Therapy can be offered in person, by video or phone call, or as an online course, either one to one or in a group.

Not in England? NHS Talking Therapies is an England service. If you live in Scotland, Wales or Northern Ireland, see "If You Live Elsewhere in the UK" below.

Who Can Self-Refer?

According to the NHS, you can usually refer yourself if you:

  • live in England
  • are 18 or over (16 or over in some areas)
  • are registered with a GP. If you're not, you may still be able to self-refer, so check your local service's website.

You don't need a diagnosis. Under-18s are usually directed to children and young people's mental health services instead.

What Can It Help With?

The NHS says you can refer yourself for help with:

  • low mood, worry and depression, including postnatal depression
  • anxiety, including generalised anxiety, social anxiety and panic disorder
  • phobias
  • obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD)
  • post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD)
  • body dysmorphic disorder
  • coping with long-term physical conditions, such as IBS or chronic pain

For some conditions, such as eating disorders, bipolar disorder, personality disorders, psychosis, and drug and alcohol problems, you'll usually need a referral from your GP to a more specialist service.

What Types of Therapy Are Offered?

NHS Talking Therapies services offer a range of NICE-recommended talking therapies. NICE is the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence, an independent organisation that produces guidelines on which treatments the NHS should offer. Depending on your needs and your local service, these may include:

  • guided self-help
  • cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT)
  • counselling
  • interpersonal therapy (IPT)
  • dynamic interpersonal therapy (DIT), a short-term psychodynamic approach
  • couples therapy
  • eye movement desensitisation and reprocessing (EMDR)
  • mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT)

You may be offered guided self-help first and moved on to another therapy if it isn't helping enough. You can say what you'd prefer, but what's available varies by area.

How to Self-Refer: Step by Step

  1. Find your local service. Use the NHS Find an NHS talking therapies service page. You'll be asked for the name of your GP surgery, which is used to find the service for your area.
  2. Fill in the referral. Most services have an online form on their website, and some also take referrals by phone. You'll usually be asked for your contact details, your GP surgery and a little about what you're struggling with.
  3. Describe what's going on in your own words. You don't need clinical terms. Say how you've been feeling, how long it's been going on and how it's affecting your life.
  4. Wait to be contacted. The service will get in touch, often to arrange an initial assessment, usually by phone or video. If they can help, they'll tell you how long you may wait for your first session.
  5. Have your assessment. A practitioner will talk with you about what's happening and what you'd like help with. You'll often be asked to fill in short questionnaires about your mood and anxiety. Together, you'll agree on the most suitable support.
  6. Start your therapy. This might be guided self-help, a course, group sessions or one-to-one therapy, in person, online or by phone.

The service will explain what information is shared with your GP.

Tips for Your Referral and Assessment

  • Be honest about how you're feeling, even if it feels hard to say. It helps the service find the right support.
  • Make a few notes beforehand: what you've noticed, how long it's been happening and what you'd like to change.
  • If you have a preference, such as one-to-one sessions, online or in-person support, or a particular type of therapy, say so.
  • Ask questions, for example how long the wait is likely to be, or what happens if the first option doesn't help.
  • If you're worried about waiting, ask whether there's anything you can do in the meantime.

Is NHS Talking Therapies Right for Me?

NHS Talking Therapies is designed for common problems like anxiety and depression, and it isn't a crisis service. It may not be the right fit if:

  • you're in crisis or feel unable to keep yourself safe (see below)
  • you have a condition that needs specialist care, such as psychosis, bipolar disorder or an eating disorder
  • you're already under the care of a specialist mental health team

If the service isn't the right fit, they'll usually tell you, and may suggest other support or speaking to your GP.

If you need help right now: if you feel unable to keep yourself safe, call 999 or go to A&E. For urgent help that isn't an emergency, call NHS 111 (in England, choose the mental health option). You can call Samaritans free on 116 123, any time of day or night.

If You Live Elsewhere in the UK

  • Wales: anyone aged 16 or over who lives in Wales, or is registered with a GP there, can sign up for free online CBT courses through SilverCloud without seeing a GP. Your GP can also refer you to local primary care mental health services.
  • Scotland: your GP is usually the first step for talking therapies. NHS inform has self-help guides, and you can call 111 for urgent mental health support.
  • Northern Ireland: speak to your GP about talking therapies and other support. For urgent help, Lifeline is available on 0808 808 8000, 24 hours a day.

Things That May Help While You Wait

  • Try free NHS self-help resources, such as Every Mind Matters
  • Keep a simple note of how you're feeling, which can be useful at your assessment
  • Stay in touch with people you trust
  • Look after the basics, such as sleep, regular meals and some physical activity
  • If things get worse while you're waiting, contact the service, your GP or NHS 111

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need to see a GP to get NHS therapy?

Not in England, for common problems like anxiety and depression. You can refer yourself to NHS Talking Therapies. It's still a good idea to see your GP if you have physical symptoms, are considering medication, or think you may need specialist care.

Is NHS Talking Therapies free?

Yes. NHS Talking Therapies is free to use.

How long is the wait?

Waiting times vary between areas. After you refer yourself, the service will contact you and, if they can help, tell you how long you may wait.

Can I choose the type of therapy?

You can say what you'd prefer, and services should take your preferences into account. But the options depend on your needs and what your local service offers.

Will my GP be told?

The service will explain what information is shared with your GP. If you have concerns, you can ask about this when you refer or at your assessment.

What if I'm not sure what's wrong?

That's fine. You don't need a diagnosis to refer yourself. Just describe what you've been experiencing, and the service will help work out what might help.
