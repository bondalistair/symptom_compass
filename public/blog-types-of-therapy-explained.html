Types of Therapy Explained: A Plain-English Guide to Your Options

By The Symptom Compass Team

CBT, psychodynamic, EMDR, ACT, counselling: the names can feel overwhelming when you just want to feel better. This guide explains the main types of therapy in plain English, how they differ, what the evidence says, and who each one might suit, so you can work out which directions are worth exploring.

If you've started looking for therapy, you've probably come across a long list of names and acronyms. It's easy to feel stuck before you've even begun, especially if you're already anxious, low or exhausted.

The good news is that you don't need to become an expert. Most types of therapy share the same basic aim: helping you understand what's happening for you and find ways to feel and cope better. Where they differ is in how they go about it.

This guide walks through the main approaches you're likely to see in the UK. It describes each one fairly, including its strengths and its limits, and says where the evidence is strong, mixed or still developing. It isn't a diagnosis, and it won't tell you which therapy you must have. It's here to help you make sense of the options.

Why Are There So Many Types of Therapy?

Different therapies grew out of different ideas about why people struggle and what helps. Some focus on the thoughts and habits that keep a problem going right now. Others explore how earlier experiences shape the way you feel and relate to people. Some teach practical skills, some work directly with distressing memories, and some focus mainly on the relationship between you and your therapist.

There's also a lot of overlap. Many therapists draw on more than one approach, and research suggests that a good working relationship with your therapist matters whichever approach is used. So the "right" therapy is often about fit: between the approach, the difficulty you're facing, the way you like to work, and the person you see.

A Few Terms Explained

  • Talking therapy. A general term for any therapy that involves talking with a trained professional. In the NHS, this is often how psychological therapies are described.
  • Counselling and psychotherapy. These terms overlap a lot. Counselling is often shorter and focused on a current difficulty, while psychotherapy is sometimes longer or explores deeper patterns. In practice, many people use the terms interchangeably.
  • NICE. The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence produces guidelines that the NHS uses to decide which treatments to offer for particular conditions. When this guide says NICE recommends something, it means for a specific condition, not for everything.
  • Evidence. When we say an approach has strong evidence, we mean it has been tested in many good-quality studies for a particular problem. Limited evidence doesn't mean a therapy doesn't work. It means it hasn't been studied as much, or the results are less clear.

The Main Types of Therapy

CBT (Cognitive Behavioural Therapy)

CBT looks at how your thoughts, feelings, physical sensations and behaviours affect each other, and how unhelpful patterns can keep problems going. It focuses mainly on the here and now. A closely related approach, behavioural activation, focuses on gradually reintroducing activities that matter to you.

What sessions are like: Usually structured, with an agenda, shared goals and practical tasks to try between sessions.

Often used for: Anxiety, depression, panic, OCD, phobias, social anxiety, trauma (in a trauma-focused form), insomnia and more.

What the evidence says: CBT has a large research base. NICE names forms of CBT as options for many conditions, including depression, generalised anxiety, social anxiety, OCD and PTSD (trauma-focused CBT).

May suit you if: you like structure, clear goals and practical tools, and you're willing to practise between sessions.

Things to bear in mind: it can feel demanding, and some people find it focuses too little on the past or on feelings they want to explore.

Psychodynamic Therapy

Psychodynamic therapy explores how past experiences, relationships and feelings you may not be fully aware of shape how you feel and behave now. It often looks at patterns that repeat in your life and relationships, including in the relationship with your therapist.

What sessions are like: More open and conversational, with you bringing what's on your mind. It can be short-term or longer-term.

Often used for: Depression, relationship difficulties, long-standing patterns, and a wish to understand yourself more deeply.

What the evidence says: The evidence base is smaller than for CBT but growing. NICE includes short-term psychodynamic psychotherapy among the treatment options for depression, and as an option for social anxiety disorder for people who decide not to have CBT or medication.

May suit you if: you want to understand the roots of your difficulties, or notice the same patterns coming up again and again.

Things to bear in mind: it's less structured, can take longer, and offers fewer specific techniques for managing symptoms day to day.

Person-Centred and Humanistic Counselling

Person-centred counselling is built on the idea that people have the capacity to grow and find their own answers when they feel genuinely accepted and understood. The counsellor offers warmth, empathy and a non-judgemental space, and follows your lead rather than directing the sessions. Other humanistic approaches, such as gestalt and integrative therapy, share a focus on the whole person.

What sessions are like: Open conversation at your own pace, without set tasks or an agenda.

Often used for: Stress, loss and bereavement, life changes, relationship worries, low self-esteem, and feeling overwhelmed.

What the evidence says: NICE includes counselling among the treatment options for depression, although the evidence is less extensive than for some structured therapies, and it isn't a first-choice option for most anxiety conditions.

May suit you if: you mainly want to be heard and supported, or you'd like space to work things out for yourself.

Things to bear in mind: it's less directive, so it may suit you less if you want practical techniques or a clear plan.

Interpersonal Therapy (IPT)

IPT focuses on the link between your mood and what's happening in your relationships and life, such as conflict, loss, role changes or isolation.

What sessions are like: Structured and time-limited, usually focusing on one or two key relationship areas.

Often used for: Mainly depression, especially when it's linked to life events or relationships.

What the evidence says: IPT is one of the treatment options for depression in NICE guidance.

May suit you if: your low mood seems tied to relationships, a loss or a big life change.

Things to bear in mind: it's mainly used for depression and is less widely available than some other therapies.

ACT (Acceptance and Commitment Therapy)

ACT helps you make room for difficult thoughts and feelings rather than struggling against them, and to take steps towards what matters most to you. It uses mindfulness, values work and practical exercises.

What sessions are like: Often structured, with exercises and metaphors, and some practice between sessions.

Often used for: Anxiety, low mood, stress, chronic pain and long-term health conditions.

What the evidence says: The evidence is growing and promising across a range of difficulties. NICE recommends ACT, alongside CBT, as an option for chronic primary pain, but it isn't a first-choice NICE option for most anxiety or depression conditions.

May suit you if: you've tried hard to push worries away without success, or you want to focus on living by your values.

Things to bear in mind: the ideas can feel abstract at first, and the research is still developing for some conditions.

Mindfulness-Based Approaches (MBCT and MBSR)

These approaches teach you to notice thoughts, feelings and body sensations with curiosity rather than getting swept up in them. Mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT) combines mindfulness with ideas from CBT.

What sessions are like: Usually group courses with weekly sessions and daily home practice.

Often used for: Preventing depression from returning, stress, and anxiety.

What the evidence says: NICE includes group mindfulness or meditation among the options for less severe depression, and MBCT is used to help people who have recovered from depression stay well.

May suit you if: you like the idea of a group course and are able to practise regularly.

Things to bear in mind: it needs regular practice, and it isn't usually the main treatment when someone is very unwell.

DBT (Dialectical Behaviour Therapy)

DBT teaches skills for managing very intense emotions, distress and relationships, while balancing acceptance with change.

What sessions are like: Often an intensive programme combining individual therapy with group skills training.

Often used for: Intense emotions, self-harm and the difficulties sometimes described as emotionally unstable or borderline personality disorder.

What the evidence says: DBT has good evidence for reducing self-harm. NICE guidance on borderline personality disorder suggests DBT may be considered where reducing self-harm is a priority.

May suit you if: your emotions feel overwhelming and hard to manage, or you're struggling with self-harm.

Things to bear in mind: it's a big commitment, and specialist programmes aren't available everywhere.

Schema Therapy

Schema therapy combines ideas from CBT, psychodynamic and other approaches. It focuses on deep-rooted patterns (schemas) that often develop in childhood and how they affect you now.

What sessions are like: Can be longer-term, mixing practical work with emotional and imagery-based techniques.

Often used for: Long-standing difficulties with relationships, self-worth and emotions, including personality difficulties.

What the evidence says: The evidence is growing, particularly for personality difficulties, but schema therapy isn't currently recommended in NICE guidelines.

May suit you if: you've had other therapy that helped only partly, or your difficulties feel deep-rooted and long-standing.

Things to bear in mind: it can be a longer commitment, and it's mostly available privately.

EMDR (Eye Movement Desensitisation and Reprocessing)

EMDR is a structured therapy for processing distressing memories. While you briefly focus on a memory, the therapist guides you through side-to-side eye movements or other rhythmic stimulation.

What sessions are like: Structured, with preparation sessions before memory processing begins. It doesn't require you to describe the trauma in great detail.

Often used for: Mainly PTSD and the effects of traumatic experiences.

What the evidence says: NICE recommends both EMDR and trauma-focused CBT as treatments for PTSD in adults.

May suit you if: you're struggling with the after-effects of a traumatic experience.

Things to bear in mind: processing memories can feel intense at first, and its use for problems other than trauma is less well researched.

Exposure Therapy

Exposure therapy helps you gradually and safely face situations, thoughts or sensations you've been avoiding, so the fear can reduce over time. It's often part of CBT.

What sessions are like: Step-by-step and planned together, starting with less difficult situations.

Often used for: Phobias, OCD (as exposure and response prevention), panic and health anxiety.

What the evidence says: Exposure-based approaches have strong evidence for phobias and OCD.

May suit you if: avoidance is a big part of what keeps your fear going.

Things to bear in mind: it can feel challenging, so it needs to be done at a pace that feels manageable, with a trained professional.

Family and Systemic Therapy

Family and systemic therapy works with families, couples or others close to you, looking at patterns between people rather than focusing on one person alone.

What sessions are like: Sessions with family members or partners, sometimes with more than one therapist.

Often used for: Family conflict, relationship difficulties, and supporting young people, for example with eating disorders.

What the evidence says: NICE recommends family therapy for some conditions, such as eating disorders in children and young people, and family intervention for psychosis.

May suit you if: the difficulties involve or affect the people around you.

Things to bear in mind: it depends on others being willing to take part, and it's less suited if you want individual space.

Somatic Approaches

Somatic therapies focus on the body: noticing physical sensations and the physical side of stress and trauma.

What sessions are like: Gentle, body-focused work, often combined with talking.

Often used for: Stress and the effects of trauma.

What the evidence says: Research is still at an early stage, and these approaches aren't currently recommended in NICE guidelines.

May suit you if: you feel your difficulties strongly in your body, or talking alone hasn't felt enough.

Things to bear in mind: there's less research behind them, and training standards vary, so it's worth checking a practitioner's qualifications carefully.

Other Support Worth Knowing About

  • Guided self-help. Workbooks, online programmes or courses, usually with some support from a practitioner. NICE often recommends these as a first step for milder anxiety and depression, and in England they're offered through NHS Talking Therapies.
  • Medication and GP support. Your GP can check for physical causes, talk through options and, where appropriate, prescribe medication such as antidepressants. For some conditions, NICE lists medication as an alternative to talking therapy, depending on what you prefer. Some people use both.

How to Choose What Might Suit You

You don't have to get it perfect first time. These questions can help you narrow things down:

  • What's troubling me most right now: worry, low mood, a traumatic memory, relationships, or something else?
  • Do I want practical tools, a space to talk, or a chance to understand where this comes from?
  • Do I prefer structure and goals, or a more open conversation?
  • How much time and energy can I give to therapy at the moment?
  • Would I consider medication, on its own or alongside therapy?
  • Do I need free NHS support, or can I look at private options?

It's also fine to ask a therapist how they work and how they'd approach your difficulty before you start. A good therapist will be honest if they think a different approach, or a different practitioner, might suit you better.

How to Access Therapy in the UK

  • Your GP can talk through your options, refer you, and discuss medication if it's relevant.
  • NHS Talking Therapies (in England) lets you refer yourself for free talking therapy for common problems like anxiety and depression. The approaches on offer vary by area. Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have their own services, which you can usually reach through your GP.
  • Private therapy gives you more choice of approach and therapist. Check that a therapist is registered with a recognised professional body, such as the BACP, UKCP, BABCP or HCPC.
  • Work, university and charities sometimes offer free or low-cost counselling.

If you need help right now: if you feel unable to keep yourself safe, call 999 or go to A&E. For urgent help that isn't an emergency, call NHS 111 (in England, choose the mental health option). You can call Samaritans free on 116 123, any time of day or night.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the most common type of therapy?

In the NHS, CBT and related approaches are among the most widely offered, partly because NICE recommends them for many common conditions. Counselling is also very common, especially through GP surgeries, workplaces and charities. Being common doesn't make an approach right for everyone.

What's the difference between counselling and therapy?

The terms overlap a lot. Counselling is often shorter and focused on a current difficulty, while psychotherapy may be longer or explore deeper patterns. What matters most is the approach used and whether it suits you.

Which type of therapy is best?

There's no single best therapy for everyone. NICE recommends particular approaches for specific conditions, such as EMDR or trauma-focused CBT for PTSD, but even then you usually have a choice. The best fit depends on your difficulty, your preferences and the therapist.

Can I choose which type of therapy I have on the NHS?

You can say what you'd prefer, and NHS services should take your preferences into account, but the options available depend on your condition, NICE guidance and what your local service offers. Private therapy gives you more choice.

What if the therapy I try doesn't help?

That's common and doesn't mean you've failed. Talk to your therapist about it. They may adjust the approach or suggest something different. Trying another approach or another therapist is a normal part of finding what works.

Is Symptom Compass a diagnosis?

No. Symptom Compass suggests therapy directions to explore based on what you describe. It isn't a diagnosis, and it doesn't replace an assessment by a qualified professional.
